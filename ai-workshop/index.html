The one idea

Every task can level up. Today you climb one rung.

You are not learning a tool. You are writing a job description for a new hire who never sleeps, then testing whether it does the work the way you would. That document is the whole workshop.

  1. RUNG 0

    Task

    Do it once, by hand. Notice every step you take and every judgment call you make.

  2. RUNG 1

    Job description

    Write the steps down: who the AI is, who it writes to, how you sound, what good looks like, what to avoid. Paste it in. Now it repeats.

    Today
  3. RUNG 2

    Agent

    Give the job description real inputs on its own: your inbox, your calendar, a form. It runs the task and you review.

  4. RUNG 3

    Automation

    Put it on a schedule or a trigger. It runs without you in the loop, and you check the output.

Rung one is where the value is. Most people skip it, copy someone else's stack, and wonder why nothing changed. The AI that changed Justin's business was not the clever one. It was the one that says "you are all over the place, focus on this." Start with your problem, not someone else's solution.

Tool map

Who makes what, and what each one is for.

Five companies, five assistants. They are more alike than different. Pick the one that lives where your work already lives, and learn it well. Your job description works in all of them.

  • ChatGPT

    by OpenAI

    Best for
    The all-rounder. Drafting, editing, brainstorming, and turning one piece of content into five. This is the room's default and the demos run here.
    Free plan
    Yes. Creating your own custom GPT or a Project needs a paid plan.
    Where your job description lives
    Free: paste it at the top of a new chat. Paid: save it as a Project or a custom GPT.

    chatgpt.com

  • Claude

    by Anthropic

    Best for
    Writing that sounds like a person. Long documents, careful edits, and holding your voice across a whole appeal without drifting.
    Free plan
    Yes, and free accounts can create a small number of Projects.
    Where your job description lives
    A Project. Paste it into the Project's instructions and every chat inside it starts from there.

    claude.ai

  • Gemini

    by Google

    Best for
    Anything that already lives in Google. Docs, Gmail, Drive, Sheets, and research that leans on Google search.
    Free plan
    Yes, and free accounts can create Gems.
    Where your job description lives
    A Gem. Same idea as a Project, and it can read files from your Drive.

    gemini.google.com

  • Copilot

    by Microsoft

    Best for
    Microsoft shops. If your day is Outlook, Word, Excel, and Teams, this is the one your IT team will say yes to.
    Free plan
    The personal Copilot app is free. Copilot inside Microsoft 365 is a paid add-on your organization has to buy.
    Where your job description lives
    Paste it at the top of a chat. If your organization has Microsoft 365 Copilot, ask IT about agents.

    copilot.microsoft.com

  • Grok

    by xAI

    Best for
    Real-time reads on what is happening on X right now. Fast, conversational, opinionated. Less useful for the writing work in this room.
    Free plan
    Yes, with limits. Not to be confused with Groq, a chip company
    Where your job description lives
    Paste it at the top of a chat.

    grok.com

Plan details checked September 2026. These companies change their free tiers often, so if a button is not where this page says it is, the feature moved, not you.

Recipe cards

Pick one track. Build one thing. Take it home.

Every card has the same shape: gather three real inputs, write the job description, test it raw versus with the job description, save it. The quiz assigned you a track. If you skipped the quiz, pick the one that names the task you dread.

Track 1

Voice

A saved instruction set that writes emails and appeals in your organization's voice, not the internet's.

Bring

Three sent emails or appeals you were proud of. Public content only.

Steps

  1. Open a fresh chat. Ask it to write a thank-you email to a first-time donor. Save that answer somewhere. That is "raw."
  2. Copy the template below. Fill in the brackets. Paste your three examples where it asks.
  3. Start a new chat, paste the whole job description, then make the exact same request.
  4. Put raw and with-job-description side by side. Run the slop check on both.
  5. Save the job description as a Project, a Gem, or a doc you paste each time.

The test: same request, with and without the voice guide. Would a donor know which one a machine wrote?

Copy the Voice job description
voice-guide.md
# [Organization name] Voice Guide

## Who you are
You draft emails and appeals on behalf of [Organization name], a [one line on what we do and for whom]. You write first drafts. A person on our team always reviews before anything is sent.

## Who we write to
[Donors, volunteers, families, community members]. They are busy, generous, and can tell when something was written by a machine.

## How we sound
- [Warm and direct. Specific over sweeping.]
- [Short sentences. Plain words. One idea per paragraph.]
- We open with the person or the moment, never with "We are excited to."
- Words we use: [list five]
- Words we never use: [list five, for example "impactful", "leverage", "journey"]

## Three examples of us at our best
Example 1:
[Paste a sent email]

Example 2:
[Paste a sent email]

Example 3:
[Paste a sent appeal]

## How to respond
When I give you a request, write it in the voice above.
- Keep it to [length, for example 120 words].
- Do not invent facts, numbers, names, or quotes. If you need one, put [NEED: description] in its place.
- Give me one subject line and one preview line at the top.
- If the request is unclear, ask me one question before writing.

Track 3

Chief of staff

A project that holds your priorities, your calendar shape, and your deadlines, and answers "what should I work on today."

Bring

This week's to-do list and your next three deadlines. Nothing confidential.

Steps

  1. Open a fresh chat. Paste your to-do list and ask "what should I work on today." Save it. That is "raw."
  2. Copy the template below. Fill in the brackets: your role, your priorities, how your week is shaped.
  3. Start a new chat, paste the whole job description, then paste the same to-do list and ask the same question.
  4. Judge the answer like you would judge a new hire's first week.
  5. Save it as a Project or a Gem. Update the priorities section every Monday.

The test: ask it Monday's question. Does it tell you what to skip, or just reorder your list?

Copy the Chief of staff job description
chief-of-staff.md
# Chief of Staff for [Your name]

## Who you are
You are chief of staff to [Your name], [role] at [Organization name]. Your job is to protect my time and keep me on the work that matters. You are direct. If I am all over the place, say so.

## What matters this quarter, in order
1. [Priority one, and what done looks like]
2. [Priority two]
3. [Priority three]
Anything not on this list is a distraction unless a deadline below forces it.

## Recurring deadlines
- [Monthly newsletter, first Tuesday]
- [Board report, the 25th]
- [Event, date]

## The shape of my week
- [Mornings are for writing. Meetings after 1 PM.]
- [Fridays are half days.]
- [I lose the most time to: email, last-minute asks, meetings without an agenda]

## Rules
- Never schedule or send anything. You recommend, I act.
- Do not invent deadlines or commitments. Ask if something is missing.
- Keep answers short. Three items max unless I ask for more.

## When I paste my to-do list and ask what to work on
1. Pick the one thing that serves the top priority and has the nearest deadline. Explain in one sentence.
2. Name two things on my list I should drop, delegate, or push, and why.
3. Flag anything on my list that is not connected to any priority above.
4. End with one question I have been avoiding.

Prompts

The prompts we run in the room.

A prompt is a request with structure. The four parts below are the whole framework. Every prompt on this page has all four, and that is the only reason they work better than "make this sound better."

  • WhoWho the AI is, and who it is writing to.
  • WhatThe task, with the real inputs pasted in.
  • Good looks likeLength, shape, tone, an example if you have one.
  • NeverWhat it must not do: invent, exaggerate, use these words.

  • The comparison test

    Run this once in a fresh chat, then once after pasting your job description. It is the first thing every track does.

    prompt 01
    Write a thank-you email to a first-time donor who gave $180 to our food pantry last week. Under 120 words. Give me a subject line.

  • Rewrite an email in our voice

    Writing and editing emails.

    prompt 02
    Here is an email I drafted. Rewrite it in the voice guide above. Keep every fact and every name exactly as written. Cut the length by a third. Open with the reader, not with us. Give me a subject line and a one-line preview text at the top.

[Paste the draft]

  • One piece, three formats

    Turning one piece of content into several.

    prompt 03
    Here is a story from our newsletter. Turn it into three things: a LinkedIn post under 150 words, an Instagram caption under 100 words with no hashtags, and a two-sentence email teaser. Same facts in all three. Do not add any claim, number, or name that is not in the original. Mark anything you are unsure about with [CHECK].

[Paste the story]

  • Headline brainstorm

    Headlines and campaign ideas.

    prompt 04
    Give me 15 headline options for [campaign, one sentence on what it is and who it is for]. Five that lead with the person helped, five that lead with a number I give you, five that lead with a question. No exclamation points. No "transform" or "impact". Then tell me which three you would test first and why, in one line each.

  • Donor-centric story

    Donor-centric storytelling.

    prompt 05
    Here are the facts about [program]. Write a 200-word story where the donor is the one who made it happen. Use "you" more than "we". One person, one moment, one detail you can see. No superlatives, no statistics I did not give you, no quotes I did not give you.

[Paste the facts as bullet points]

  • Read the numbers

    Analyzing marketing metrics. Bring a clean export, not a screenshot.

    prompt 06
    Attached is a spreadsheet of [last quarter's email results, one row per send]. Tell me the three things that changed the most between the first month and the last. For each one, give me the numbers you compared, one plausible explanation, and one question you would ask before drawing a conclusion. Show your math. Do not recommend anything yet.

  • Slop check

    Run this on anything before it goes out.

    prompt 07
    Read this as a long-time donor who has received fifty of our emails. What in it sounds generic, machine-made, or like every other nonprofit? Quote the exact phrases. Then rewrite only those phrases and leave everything else untouched.

[Paste the draft]

  • Fix a hedged answer

    For the topics where models flatten or hedge: Israel, antisemitism, security. Context fixes tone.

    prompt 08
    Your last draft hedged. We are a Jewish community organization writing to our own community about [topic]. Our readers do not need both sides explained; they need to hear us speak plainly and with care. Rewrite it in our voice guide, drop every "some may feel" and "it is important to note", and keep the facts I gave you exactly as they are.

This is the version one list. The prompts the room writes together on the day get added here afterward, so the page you leave with is better than the page you arrived at.

The slop check

Would a donor know?

Slop in an appeal is not embarrassing. It is expensive. Six questions, thirty seconds, before anything goes out. If you answer no to any of them, it is not done.

  1. Would a long-time donor believe a person wrote this?Read it out loud. If you would not say it across a table, cut it.
  2. Is every fact, number, name, and quote yours?The model will invent a statistic to fill a gap. Check each one against the source.
  3. Did it use a word your team never uses?"Impactful", "leverage", "journey", "thrilled". Keep a banned list in your job description.
  4. Does it open with the reader, not the organization?"You made this happen" beats "We are excited to share".
  5. Could you cut a third and lose nothing?Then cut a third. Models pad by default.
  6. Did a person read it before it went out?Every job description on this page says the AI drafts and a person approves. That line is not decoration.

Justin's stack

What runs a one-person business, and why each piece earns its place.

Not a recommendation list. This is the actual setup, shown so you can see how rung one becomes rung three. Copy the shape, not the tools.

  • Meetings

    Granola

    Sits in every call and writes the notes. Afterward the transcript syncs into the same place the AI tools read from.

    Every client conversation becomes a source of truth the AI can work from. No retyping, no "what did we agree to."

  • Voice to text

    Wispr Flow

    Hold a key, talk, and clean text lands wherever the cursor is. Emails, briefs, prompts.

    You talk faster than you type. This page started as a four-minute voice note, and that note was the brief.

  • The build layer

    Claude Code and Codex

    Where a job description becomes an agent that reads the inbox, drafts the reply, files the meeting notes, and builds the dashboard. Rung two and three.

    Nothing sends without a review. The drafts land in a folder, a person approves, then it goes. Same rule as your voice guide, at scale.

  • Connectors

    Gmail, Calendar, Drive, Slack

    The AI reads the calendar, the inbox, and the files it is allowed to see, and nothing else. Permissions are given per tool, on purpose.

    The three-minute demo in the room: one sentence in, a finished Google Form out. That is rung two, and it is closer than it looks.
